US Wildfires: the highs are higher.
So are the lows.

Annual acres burned haven't just trended up. Across four decades of the official record, the whole distribution has shifted — the biggest fire years are bigger, and the quiet years are no longer as quiet.

This site reads the American fire record through three lenses. The first is 2.3 million individual federal fire records from 1992 to 2020 — Karen Short's FPA-FOD, the per-incident spine of everything below. The second is the official National Interagency Fire Center national totals, 1983 through 2025 — the number every headline quotes, and the series NIFC itself considers reliable only from 1983 forward [NIFC]. The third is NOAA's divisional climate record, the drought and temperature backdrop against which those fires burned.

It extends a 2023 analysis whose own limitations slide named weather as the missing variable. That earlier capstone charted the rise in acres burned but stopped at the trend line; here the same question is pushed further — into the shape of the distribution, and toward a bounded model that finally puts drought on the same axis as fire. Where that lineage matters, it is disclosed, including the correction of a labeling bug that mislabeled the 1992–2020 records as “1990–2022.”

What you will find is a claim defended in stages. The published literature — the EPA's climate indicators [epa2021wildfires] and the Fifth National Climate Assessment [nca5_focus_wildfire] — establishes that the largest fire years cluster in recent decades. The rising floor, the widening band between quiet and catastrophic years, is this project's own analysis of the NIFC and FPA-FOD series, presented as such. From there the site walks through the trend, the seasons, the geography, the drought–fire link, and a deliberately modest predictive model — each with its uncertainties in view.
