Seasonality · when the country burns

Two calendars, two fire seasons

US wildfire has a rhythm, and it is really two rhythms stacked on top of each other. People start fires at the edges of the year — spring and fall — while lightning concentrates its work into July and August. And geographically, the Southeast's spring season and the West's summer season are almost mirror images. All figures below are FPA-FOD discovery-month counts, 1992–2020. [short2022fpafod]

§1 Who burns which month

Split every fire by its NWCG cause — Human, Natural (lightning), or Missing/undetermined — and lay the twelve months across. The two phase against each other: human ignition counts peak in March–April and stay high through the fall, while natural ignitions are near-zero from November through April and then dominate July–August.

The reading, confirmed in the data

Human ignitions dominate the calendar edges (spring and fall); lightning owns July–August. Human fire counts peak at 256,679 in April and 251,029 in March, staying elevated through October. Natural (lightning) fires bottom out near 931 in January and peak at 105,950 in July and 99,527 in August — more than a hundredfold seasonal swing.

§2 The country keeps two fire seasons

Break the same counts out by state and the single national calendar splits in two. The Southeast burns in spring — Georgia, South Carolina, and the Gulf states peak in February–April, driven by human ignition in cured winter fuels — while the West burns in summer, with California, Oregon, Idaho, and the Great Basin peaking in July–August under lightning and drought. The reading, confirmed in the data

Southeast spring vs West summer — two fire seasons, out of phase. Georgia's fire count peaks in March (33,964) and South Carolina in March (18,485); both fall away by summer. California peaks in July (53,011) and August (41,887), Oregon in July–August (20,697 / 22,849), and Idaho in August (17,455) — exactly the months the Southeast has gone quiet.
