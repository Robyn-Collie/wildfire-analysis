The largest fires

Class G fires: 0.2% of ignitions, 75% of the acres

0.2% of fires. 75% of the acres.

Size class G — 5,000 acres and up — is 4,783 fires out of 2,303,566 in the record: about 0.2% of them. Those few burned 135.1 million acres — roughly 75% of every acre this record counts.

The driver

Drought and burned area

Rank each state's years by growing-season drought and by how much it burned, and in most states the two rankings track. Across all 38 qualifying states the median correlation is −0.45, and 15 clear the strong-negative bar. Colorado is the headline: ρ = −0.72 — its driest growing seasons are almost exactly its worst fire years.

In the Drought overlay chapter above, set the year to 2020: California's Palmer Drought Severity Index falls to −3.89 by December — severe drought — and 2020 is one of only three years in the official record to exceed ten million acres burned.

The wrinkle: fuel-limited deserts break the rule

If drought drove fire everywhere, the drought–fire correlation would be strongly negative in every state. It is not. Two of the driest states in the country sit at the bottom of the list:

  • Arizona: ρ = −0.18 (not significant, p = 0.36)
  • Nevada: ρ = +0.13 — the wrong sign entirely (p = 0.50)

The rest of the interior West behaves as expected — Colorado −0.72, New Mexico −0.52, California −0.51, Wyoming −0.46 — with the correlation weakening as you move into the drier, sparser Great Basin (Idaho −0.33, Utah −0.28) until it inverts in Arizona and Nevada.

The likely reason is that these deserts are fuel-limited, not flammability-limited. In a Colorado forest, the fuel is always there; what varies year to year is whether it is dry enough to burn, so drought and fire move together. In the Nevada desert, the fuel — fine grasses between the shrubs — is not always there. A wet year grows the grass; the following dry year burns it. Fire chases the wet year, not the dry one, which pushes the same-year drought–fire correlation toward zero or positive.

This flammability-limited vs. fuel-limited split is observed in this data (the AZ/NV sign reversal above) and is consistent with the fuel-limitation mechanism described in the fire-ecology literature. We did not model the one-year lag here; the pattern is presented as an observed exception with a literature-consistent explanation, not a fitted result.
