Technical note

Methods & provenance

Every chart on this site is built from three public federal datasets. This page is the full account: where the data came from, how it is licensed, what it can't tell you, and exactly how it was processed. Nothing here is proprietary; everything is reproducible.

02Caveats — read before trusting any number

These are not fine print. Each one bounds a claim somewhere on this site, and each is documented from the source datasets themselves.

Containment date is 39% null — no duration analysis

The CONT_DATE field is missing for roughly 39% of FPA-FOD records. Any analysis of fire duration or containment time would be biased by which fires happen to record an end date, so this site does not run duration analyses. Where fire “length” might otherwise be implied, it is scoped out.

The 8% “missing cause” is a real category, not dirty data

About 8% of records carry no human/natural cause. This is not a cleaning artifact: the FPA-FOD cause field is the NWCG standard (approved August 2020), whose broad classification is literally three values — Human, Natural, Missing data/not specified/undetermined. The undetermined bucket is a native, first-class category of the dataset. This is why the human-caused share reported here (77% of all records) sits below Balch et al. (2017)'s published 84%: Balch computes the human share among cause-attributed fires only [balch2017]. Both are correct under their own denominators.

No data before 1983 — by NIFC's design

The trend line starts in 1983 because NIFC's own record does. Verbatim:

NIFC, verbatim

The National Interagency Coordination Center at NIFC compiles annual wildland fire statistics for federal and state agencies. This information is provided through Situation Reports, which have been in use for several decades. Prior to 1983, the federal wildland fire agencies did not track official wildfire data using current reporting processes. As a result, there is no official data prior to 1983 posted on this site.

Pre-1983 figures are not dropped here — they never existed in NIFC's product. This site states that rather than implying an omission.

1983–1984 fire counts are undercounts

The first two years of the modern series report anomalously low fire counts (18,229 and 20,493) against the ~70,000–90,000 typical of later years, while acres are normal. This is early Situation-Report undercounting of ignitions, not a real collapse. Acres for 1983–1984 are fine to chart; the fire-count line is flagged or started later to avoid implying a spurious jump.

2004 acres exclude North Carolina state lands

NIFC's own footnote (verbatim): 2004 fires and acres do not include state lands for North Carolina. The 2004 total is therefore slightly low — immaterial to the trend, but labeled wherever 2004 is annotated.

2024 fire count: NIFC disagrees with itself

NIFC's live “Wildfires and Acres” table lists 64,897 fires for 2024; the 2025 Annual Report narrative says 67,897 — a difference of exactly 3,000 that looks like a digit transposition (64,897 vs 67,897). Acres for 2024 agree across both sources (8,924,884). This site uses 64,897, from the canonical statistics table, and discloses the report's figure rather than silently choosing one.

FPA-FOD undercounts nonfederal activity

The per-fire dataset is not a complete census. Verbatim from its metadata: Viable state and local (i.e., nonfederal) records were not available from all states for all years, and as a result the data may therefore underrepresent actual wildfire activity in certain areas and time periods. Every state-level or count-based claim on this site inherits this caveat. FPA-FOD (per-fire, 1992–2020) and NIFC (national totals, 1983–2025) are also different measurements — different scopes, denominators, and start years — so their acre sums differ slightly year to year; neither is “wrong.”

PDSI is a blunt drought instrument

The drought covariate in the model is the Palmer Drought Severity Index, which has well-documented limits — stated here so the drought→fire link is not overclaimed:

  • Slow response (~9-month timescale). PDSI lags rapidly emerging drought and performs poorly on flash drought and short-term soil-moisture swings; it best captures established, long-term dry or wet spells. Source: UCAR/NCAR Climate Data Guide, Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI) [pdsi-cdg].
  • Calibration sensitivity + weak standardization. PDSI values depend on the calibration period, and the standardization is “only weakly justified on physical or statistical grounds”; drought-termination rules can retroactively shift prior-month values. Source: Alley (1984) [alley1984].
  • Warming-era bias. Its temperature-driven (Thornthwaite) potential-evapotranspiration term can overstate drying under warming unless self-calibrated / Penman-Monteith variants are used. Source: Dai, Trenberth & Qian (2004) [dai2004].

PDSI is a defensible contemporaneous drought covariate at monthly/seasonal, regional resolution — but not a fire-spread predictor and not a sub-seasonal forecast input. See the model page's honesty block.

03Lineage: the sequel to a limitations slide

This site is the sequel to a 2023 Georgia analytics capstone project. That project charted the rise in US wildfire acreage and, to its credit, ended on a limitations slide that named the missing piece out loud: it had no weather variable. This site is the answer to that slide — the same fire record, now joined to NOAA's climate data so the drought→fire relationship can actually be tested rather than gestured at.

A correction the original got wrong

The 2023 capstone labeled its data “1990–2022.” That labeling is incorrect. The FPA-FOD 6th edition covers 1992–2020 — the sheet titles were mislabeled. Every date range on this site reflects the true span. Carrying an inherited error forward silently would defeat the entire point of a methods page, so it is corrected here explicitly.

This site was built by Robyn Collie. It reuses the fire story of the 2023 capstone but is an independent, re-sourced, re-validated analysis.

04Pipeline

Raw federal data flows through a small, named set of scripts into the JSON files this site fetches at runtime. The path is: original archive (Tableau .twbx → extracted .hyper → Parquet) and fixed-width NOAA text, into a merged panel, into per-page JSON.

  1. pipeline/extract_fires.py — reads the FPA-FOD per-fire records (from the archived Tableau extract, via .hyper → Parquet) and aggregates to state × year fire counts and acres.
  2. pipeline/parse_climate.py — parses NOAA nClimDiv fixed-width files, replaces element-specific missing-value sentinels with NaN, filters to the 50 states (nClimDiv state codes are not FIPS), and aggregates the May–Oct season (PDSI/temp mean, precip sum).
  3. pipeline/build_site_data.py — joins fire and climate on state × year and writes the site-data JSON files (the fire-climate panel, national annual totals, seasonality, map grid, cause/class breakdowns).
  4. analysis/trend_stats.py — computes the trend statistics: era comparison, rolling percentile band, Theil-Sen / Mann-Kendall / Mann-Whitney tests, the 10-million-acre club, and fire-climate correlations → trend_stats.json.
  5. analysis/model.py — fits the pooled OLS seasonal-risk model with leave-one-year-out cross-validation and writes model_results.json (the numbers behind the Model page).
The audit convention: every number is traceable

Every statistic printed on this site is either loaded at runtime from /data/*.json or carries an inline <!-- STAT: file.path --> comment in the page source, pointing at the exact JSON path it came from. A verification pass audits each STAT comment against its source file. No number on this site was typed from memory; if it isn't traceable to a source JSON, it doesn't ship.
