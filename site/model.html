Prediction: which new fires become large?

A model that ranks newly discovered fires by how likely they are to reach 300 acres, using only what is known on the discovery day. On fires from 2019 and 2020 that it never saw, the model's top tenth of scores held of the fires that went on to reach 300 acres, against for a lookup of each place's historical rate by month. Trained on 2010-2018, tested once on 2019-2020 (locked holdout). Where a fire starts (its fuels, terrain and land status) carries the ranking; weather on the discovery day adds nothing measurable.

These are ranks, not probabilities. For the tenth of 2019-2020 fires it scored highest, the model's mean score was ; the share that actually reached 300 acres was . Read as probabilities, the scores do worse than a single constant rate (Brier skill ), mostly in 2019, a quiet year after two large seasons ( in 2019, in 2020). So this page shows how well the model orders fires and never a percent chance for a fire.

Coverage. Trained on 706,554 CONUS fires discovered 2010-2018 and tested on the 135,685 discovered 2019-2020. Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico are excluded (12,641 fires) because the weather and fuels attributes cover CONUS only; Alaska holds 19% of 2010-2020 Class G acres, so nothing here applies to it. Where a state's records come mostly from local reporters with no recorded cause (Texas, Kansas), the cause input is a reporter indicator rather than a cause.

Metrics with baselines

Every row uses the same test fires and the same interval method. Baselines are always shown with the model. The model wins on the ranking metrics (PR-AUC, ROC-AUC) and loses on Brier skill, which scores the numbers as probabilities.

Where it does not work

  • It ranks Southern fires worse than the place-and-month lookup, except in Texas ().
  • Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico are outside it.
  • Owner, reporting agency and protection status carry much of the ranking: they record where fires are fought, reported and allowed to burn, not only how they behave. A place's score says nothing about the quality of its land management.
  • It is not for any decision about an actual fire. The full list is in the model card.

The previous duration model was retired

The earlier version of this project fitted a Random Forest to predict how many days a fire would burn from its location, day of year and recorded cause. It reproduces exactly, and it is retired because it is not useful:

  • On mean absolute error it loses to predicting zero days for every fire: against days.
  • of fires are contained on the day they are found, so a constant is a strong baseline that any model has to beat before it says anything.
  • Its sample dropped of fires from 2010 on because they lack a containment date, and unevenly: of Texas records are missing it. The containment date is a reporting-system convention, not a measure of the fire (see Ownership).
