California: when and where do power-line ignitions become large fires?

The question a utility wildfire team might ask: when and where do our highest-risk ignitions start, and what does the public record not tell us? This page answers it for California from the FPA FOD record of 1992-2020. It describes fires, not any utility.

Can a model rank new ignitions by how likely they are to get large?

The site's ranking model was trained on 2010-2018 fires and tested on 2019-2020 fires it had not seen. In California, test fires, of which reached 300 acres: the tenth of fires the model ranked highest held of the fires that reached 300 acres, against for a lookup of the usual rate by place and month.

The scores are ranks, not probabilities, and the model sees only the cause class (human, natural, unknown), not "power line". It says which ignitions deserve attention first; it does not say why a fire started or whether any equipment was involved.

What the record does not tell you

  • Undercount. The general cause is missing for of California's fires; some of those are power-line fires.
  • Mechanism. One code covers vegetation contact, equipment failure, conductor clash and animal contact, and transmission, distribution and generation alike. Which of these started a fire is the question a utility most needs answered, and this record cannot answer it.
  • Exposure. There is no denominator: the record has no line miles, no vegetation near lines and no outage or de-energization data, so it cannot say whether one area's lines are riskier than another's.
  • Weather. The record has no wind or humidity, so this page cannot say whether wind explains the months above.
  • Time. The record ends in 2020.
